The New Travel Gatekeeper

When AI shapes the shortlist, will it find your Lapland product?

1 AUGUST 20266 MIN READBALAI
In this insight

  2. Availability vs visibility
  3. Hidden journey
  4. Discovery and trust
  5. Why Lapland
  6. Commercial response
  7. BALAI’s view

A tourism product can be available, suitable and genuinely excellent—and still be absent from the traveller’s decision.

Travel discovery is no longer taking place only through search engines, social media, booking platforms and travel agencies. Travellers are beginning to ask artificial-intelligence tools where to go, what to experience and whether a destination can meet their individual requirements.

This creates a new, largely invisible layer between the traveller and the tourism company. It can influence which products enter consideration, which appear credible and which never reach the shortlist.

Discovery no longer begins with a list of links

A traveller can now describe an entire journey in one request: who is travelling, when they want to go, what they value and what conditions the trip must satisfy.

The AI system does not simply present a list of websites. It interprets the request and assembles a possible journey from the information it can access and understand.

The Mastercard–CrescentRating Global Muslim Travel Index 2026 reports that 80 percent of travellers covered by its research now use AI tools for travel purposes. It also warns that services can exist physically while remaining difficult to discover during AI-assisted planning.

That distinction is especially relevant for travellers who need confidence that particular cultural, religious or practical requirements can be met before committing to a long-distance journey.

The implication is not that AI has replaced travel agencies or conventional search. It means that part of the decision may already have been shaped before a tourism company—or even a travel-trade partner—receives the first enquiry.

Availability is not visibility

A Lapland company may already be capable of accommodating a particular customer. Its staff may be flexible, its experience may be suitable and the necessary arrangements may be entirely possible.

But the market cannot evaluate what it cannot clearly understand.

If the available information does not communicate the product accurately, an AI system may omit it, interpret it incorrectly or favour an alternative that appears easier to verify.

A product can be ready on the ground while remaining invisible at the point of decision.

This does not mean every tourism company must become a technology company. It means the gap between what a business can deliver and what another market can confidently understand is becoming more consequential.

The booking arrives after the decision has begun

An AI-influenced reservation will not necessarily arrive labelled as one.

The supplier sees the final booking relationship. It may never see the questions that shaped the traveller’s decision—or why one product entered the shortlist while another disappeared.

BALAI’s first insight, The Market Behind the Middleman, examined how a tourism company may not see the true source market behind a booking.

The visibility problem can also operate in the opposite direction: the traveller may not clearly see the right Lapland product before the booking exists.

AI changes discovery—but trust still closes the distance

Research does not suggest that travellers have abandoned familiar booking channels.

Expedia Group’s 2026 AI Trust Gap study, involving more than 5,700 adults in the United States, United Kingdom and India, found that only eight percent of respondents in the US and UK currently relied on AI tools when planning a trip.

However, the same research found meaningful openness to AI-assisted suggestions and itinerary building. When it came to completing the purchase, 68 percent still preferred a trusted travel brand over an AI assistant.

These findings are not contradictory. They show a fragmented customer journey.

AI may influence discovery without controlling the booking. A traveller can use it to identify possibilities and then turn to an agency, operator, platform or direct human contact for confirmation.

The rise of AI therefore does not make travel-trade relationships less important. It can make the combination of digital visibility and trusted human verification more valuable.

A product must first enter consideration. It must then survive evaluation, packaging and confirmation.

Why this matters in Lapland

Lapland does not suffer from a complete absence of international awareness. Its winter landscapes, Northern Lights and Arctic experiences already have powerful global appeal.

The harder commercial question is whether individual products are being understood and selected by the right markets.

According to Visit Finland’s results for the 2025–2026 winter season, the number of international travellers choosing Lapland as their main destination increased by 14 percent, while their total spending in Lapland declined by five percent.

These figures do not establish any connection between AI and visitor spending. They underline a broader point: greater visitor volume does not automatically produce greater locally captured value.

Visibility must therefore be commercially meaningful—not merely broad.

For Southeast Asian travellers, a journey to Lapland can involve substantial distance, cost and logistical complexity. Unresolved uncertainty can remove a product from consideration even when the company would have been capable of serving the customer.

The most suitable experience is not always the one that appears easiest to understand from another market.

The answer is not to chase every AI platform

An AI system can process information, but it does not replace market understanding. It cannot reliably communicate what a tourism company has never clarified, resolve a cultural uncertainty that the available information does not recognise or determine commercial suitability with the judgement of an experienced market partner.

The question is therefore no longer only: Can customers find our website?

It is increasingly: Can the market correctly understand what we offer, who it suits and whether it can be trusted?

That question sits at the intersection of market knowledge, cultural interpretation, product communication and commercial distribution. The answer will differ between markets. Information that creates confidence in Finland may not address the questions being asked in Indonesia. Material written for an independent traveller may not provide what a travel-trade partner needs to evaluate the same product.

The detailed assessment of those differences remains specific to the company, product and intended market.

Tourism companies do not need to redesign their business around every new technology, nor publish every operational detail in an attempt to appear everywhere.

The more durable objective is to reduce the gap between the experience a company can genuinely deliver and the way that experience is interpreted beyond its home market.

Technology may influence the first shortlist. Trusted partners still assess feasibility, assemble itineraries, confirm requirements and carry responsibility when circumstances change.

The strongest position therefore comes from being visible enough to enter consideration, clear enough to be evaluated and credible enough to be carried forward by people the traveller trusts.

BALAI’s view

The next visibility gap in tourism may not result from an absence of demand or suitable products. It may emerge because the two are being interpreted through systems that neither side can fully see.

For Lapland companies seeking Southeast Asian markets, the objective should not be to say more everywhere. It should be to ensure that the right product is understood by the right market—and supported by the right commercial relationships.

As travel discovery changes, that ability becomes part of market access itself.
